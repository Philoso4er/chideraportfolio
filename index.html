How I work

A project management approach to building products

I don't present myself as a software developer. My role on these products is the one a project lead or product owner plays: deciding what gets built and why, in what order, with which suppliers, and checking it works before it ships.

01

Scoping and requirements

Turning an idea into a defined problem, a target user and a written set of rules and features before the build starts.

Example: Pixel Art Grid's "hard rules": every pixel costs exactly £1, with no tiers, auctions, subscriptions or ads.

02

MoSCoW-style prioritisation

Separating what must ship from what should, could or won't (yet), and keeping a visible backlog rather than letting scope creep.

Example: Checkout's Business plan is shown as "coming soon" rather than built; InHouse documents what is done and what is still a placeholder.

03

Stakeholder and user thinking

Designing around specific people and situations, and tailoring the work to who needs to approve it.

Example: the 60-Second Puzzle Fix was framed as a concept pitch for one named media business; Pawesome includes a sitter handoff sheet and a senior-pet journal.

04

Risk and controls

Identifying what could go wrong (cost, misuse, bad data, unsafe advice) and building a control in, not bolting it on later.

Example: InHouse recalculates every price on the server; Checkout caps free usage; Pawesome screens forum posts with AI moderation.

05

Integration planning

Choosing third-party services, mapping how data and money flow between them, and setting up keys, webhooks and environments.

Services used: Stripe, Supabase, Firebase, Google Gemini, Printify, WebXR.

06

Testing, iteration and deployment

Checking each release against intended functionality, diagnosing failures end to end, and shipping through GitHub and Vercel.

Example: InHouse pricing was recalibrated after real test orders showed large-print shipping was far higher than a simple ratio assumed.

Case study 01 · AI product research

Checkout: an AI product-research assistant

An assistant that helps shoppers make better-informed buying decisions. You give it a product as a link, a photo or a description, then ask questions and get neutral answers with source links.

My role
Project lead / product owner: problem definition, feature scope, integrations, testing and release
Integrations
Google Gemini (with Google Search grounding), Stripe subscriptions, Vercel serverless and edge functions
Status
Live, with a free tier and a paid Pro plan
View live app (Checkout, opens in a new tab)

1The problem

Researching a purchase means piecing together specs, reviews and prices from many sites, a lot of which are written to sell. The goal was a single place to ask factual questions about one specific product and see where the answers came from.

2The user

Shoppers comparing a product before they buy, especially in categories where specs matter: phones, laptops, graphics cards, TVs and audio, and kitchen appliances. The pricing separates casual shoppers (free) from frequent "power shoppers" (Pro).

3Research and planning

I defined the core journey first: provide a product, let the assistant identify it and confirm, then ask follow-up questions. A neutral tone was a requirement from the start, so the assistant is told to avoid promotional language and opinions. The product went through several versions before the current release, which is built around Gemini.

4Scope and feature decisions

Must
Link, image or text input; neutral answers; source links
Should
Free tier limited to 9 queries a month; Pro plan at £4.99/month with a self-service billing portal; privacy and terms pages
Could
A "mood" indicator that gives the assistant some personality
Later
Business plan (embed on a store, custom branding, analytics), shown as "coming soon" rather than built

5Integrations

  • Google Gemini 2.5 Flash with Google Search grounding, called through a server-side route so the API key never reaches the browser.
  • A search aggregator that queries free public sources (general web search, news, Wikipedia, Reddit) and specialist sites for phones, laptops and price comparison, with time-outs and a cap on results per site so no single source dominates.
  • Stripe Checkout, webhooks and the customer portal for the Pro subscription.

6Testing and iteration

Releases were checked against the intended journey for each input type. The product handles failure cases on purpose: a clear message when the free limit is reached, a separate "at capacity, try again" response when the AI service is rate-limited, and time-outs so a slow source can't stall an answer.

7Deployment

Deployed on Vercel, with serverless functions for the AI, usage and payment routes and an edge function for search. Keys for Gemini and Stripe are held as environment variables, and a signed Stripe webhook endpoint receives subscription events.

8Lessons learned

When every answer costs money to generate, usage limits and a paid tier belong in the first scope, not in a later phase. Holding back the Business plan kept the release focused on the core shopper journey.

Case study 02 · Consumer app, multi-feature

Pawesome: an AI companion for pet owners

A single app for pet owners: an AI vet-style assistant by text, voice or photo, live camera consultations, health, feeding and training records, breed-specific training guides, and a community forum and feed.

My role
Project lead / product owner: scope, data model, integrations, testing and release
Integrations
Firebase Authentication and Cloud Firestore; Google Gemini (chat, vision, transcription, Live API)
Status
Live (sign in with Google)
View live app (Pawesome, opens in a new tab)

1The problem

Pet owners keep advice, vet history, feeding routines and training progress in different places, or nowhere. The product promise was "the only app a pet owner should ever need": one place where everything is tied to the pet it belongs to.

2The user

Pet owners of any species, with specific situations designed in: handing a pet over to a sitter, caring for an older pet, training a new dog, or wanting a quick answer before deciding whether to see a vet.

3Research and planning

The plan was built around one data model: a user owns pets, and each pet has its own chats, health records, feeding logs, training progress and journal entries. That model is written down as a blueprint and enforced in the database rules, and it means the pet's name, breed and age can be passed into every AI answer.

4Scope and feature decisions

  • Core: AI assistant (text, voice notes, photos), pet profiles with breed recognition from a photo, health diary, feeding tracker.
  • Engagement: breed-specific training guides that can be saved as a PDF, video feedback on training practice, community forum, social feed and playdates.
  • Situational tools: a sitter handoff sheet and a senior-pet journal that builds a summary to share with a vet.
  • Safety rule: the assistant always recommends a vet for serious medical concerns.

5Integrations

  • Firebase: Google sign-in, and Cloud Firestore for profiles, records and community posts, with security rules that check ownership and validate every field.
  • Google Gemini: chat with Google Search grounding, image analysis, audio transcription, structured outputs (breed recognition, content moderation) and the Live API for real-time voice and camera consultations.

6Testing and iteration

The deal finder shows the iteration loop: it failed for less common product searches, so the root cause was written up and the feature redesigned as a two-step search-then-structure process, with direct retailer search links as a fallback so users never hit a dead end. Forum posts are screened by AI moderation before publishing, and an error boundary stops one failing feature from taking down the app.

7Deployment

Prototyped in Google AI Studio, then deployed to Vercel, with Firebase as the backend. Users sign in with Google, and each person's pets and records are stored under their own account.

8Lessons learned

Breadth is expensive. Ten-plus features each need testing and maintenance, so the next iteration should go deeper on the core (assistant and records) rather than wider. Settling the data model early made adding features far easier.

Case study 03 · Payments and data

Pixel Art Grid: £1 pixels on a shared canvas

A shared one-million-pixel canvas where people buy pixels for £1 each and add a colour plus an optional name, message and link. "Leave your mark on the internet."

My role
Project lead / product owner: product rules, payment flow, integrations, diagnosis and release
Integrations
Stripe (Payment Intents, Elements, webhooks), Supabase (Postgres), Vercel serverless functions
Status
Live
View live app (Pixel Art Grid, opens in a new tab)

1The problem

Give anyone a simple, permanent and affordable way to leave a visible mark online, with a proposition that fits on one line: one million pixels, £1 each, choose a colour, leave your mark.

2The user

Individuals who want a novelty or personal keepsake, and people who want a pixel that links somewhere, such as a project or small business. Buyers need a fast checkout, and the owner needs a way to manage orders.

3Research and planning

The product's "hard rules" were written down and kept: every pixel costs exactly £1, with no premium pixels, dynamic pricing, tiers, NFTs, crypto, auctions, subscriptions, memberships or ads. Changes are recorded in a dated product-updates log alongside a "next" backlog.

4Scope and feature decisions

Must
Select pixels, pick any colour, pay £1 per pixel; only colour is required
Should
Multi-pixel selection with a running "Buy now: £N" total; same or individual settings per pixel; share links; a page per pixel
Could
Activity feed, recently claimed, leaderboard, FAQ, downloadable pixel card, draw mode (backlog)
Won't
Tiers, auctions, crypto, subscriptions or ads (ruled out by the product rules)

5Integrations

  • Stripe for card and redirect-based payments, with a webhook that marks pixels as sold once payment succeeds.
  • Supabase for pixel and order records.
  • Admin area with a dashboard, order list and details, pixel management, and a clean-up job for expired unpaid orders.
  • The payments setup moved to Stripe from an earlier configuration that used Flutterwave and PayPal.

6Testing and iteration

A follow-up release restored checkout behaviour that users expected from an earlier version (full colour picker, multi-select, optional fields). When payments stopped working, the live site was checked step by step. The fault was traced upstream of Stripe to an unreachable database, and a missing confirmation step for redirect-based payments was found and added.

7Deployment

Vercel hosts the front end and API routes, with routing rules for shareable pixel pages. Environment variables are split between public (browser) and server-only keys, and the Stripe webhook endpoint is registered against the live domain.

8Lessons learned

A "payment problem" isn't always a payment problem. Diagnosing the whole chain (browser, API, database, payment provider) found the real cause quickly. Free-tier services can pause, so production needs monitoring. Writing the product rules down stopped scope drifting into tiers and upsells.

Case study 04 · E-commerce and fulfilment

InHouse: see your art on the wall, then order it

Upload artwork, pick a size and frame, preview it on your own wall through the phone camera, pay, and the order goes automatically to a print-on-demand partner for printing and shipping.

My role
Project lead / product owner: order flow, pricing model, supplier integration, testing and release
Integrations
Stripe Checkout and webhooks, Printify API (print and fulfilment), device camera, Vercel functions
Status
Live; README tracks remaining go-live steps
View live app (InHouse, opens in a new tab)

1The problem

It's hard to judge how a print will look on your wall before you buy it, and getting your own image printed, framed and delivered usually means several separate steps. InHouse joins preview, payment and fulfilment into one flow.

2The user

People who want their own artwork or photos printed and framed for their home, and want to check size and frame colour in the actual room first. Shipping is set up for the UK, US, Canada, Australia and Ireland.

3Research and planning

I mapped the end-to-end order flow and the suppliers involved: Stripe for payment and the shipping address, Printify for production and delivery. The pricing model was defined as live production cost + shipping per size + 20% VAT + a flat margin.

4Scope and feature decisions

  • In scope: upload, 3 sizes × 3 frame colours, camera preview you can drag, resize and rotate, live pricing, checkout, automatic order submission.
  • Logged as known gaps: alerting if an order fails after payment, a low-resolution image warning, a customer order-status page, tax and wider international shipping.

5Integrations

  • Stripe Checkout collects payment and the shipping address. A webhook listens for completed payments.
  • Printify: on payment, the image is uploaded and the order created automatically, using a specific framed-poster product and print provider.
  • Pricing control: the price is recalculated on the server at checkout, so a price changed in the browser can never be charged.

6Testing and iteration

Pricing was checked against real Printify test orders, placed with the payment method removed so nothing was charged. They showed shipping for the largest size was about three times the production cost, against roughly 1.6× for the smaller sizes, so one flat ratio would have badly underpriced large prints. Shipping is now set per size, and VAT was confirmed at 20%. An earlier version timed out on Vercel, so a slow setup step was moved into a one-off script.

7Deployment

Vercel serves the static front end and the API routes for pricing, image upload, checkout and the webhook. The README lists the remaining go-live steps: a supplier payment method, switching Stripe to live keys, and confirming a real order end to end.

8Lessons learned

Test cost assumptions with real data before charging customers. Writing an honest "done vs placeholder" list keeps everyone clear on what can and can't be relied on, and it doubles as the go-live checklist.

Case study 05 · AR and conversational AI

Hi, I'm an Alien: an AR web game

An alien appears through your phone camera, stands on real surfaces and chats with you in character. It runs in the browser, so there's no app to install.

My role
Project lead / product owner: game concept, device strategy, integrations, testing and release
Integrations
Google Gemini (character dialogue), WebXR (floor detection), device camera, Web Speech API, three.js
Status
Live; best on a phone
View live app (Hi, I'm an Alien, opens in a new tab)

1The problem

Most AR experiences need an app download before anything happens. The aim was a short, surprising "micro-moment", meeting a character that seems to be in your room, that works straight from a link.

2The user

Casual mobile players driven by curiosity rather than progression. The design principles were written down: not a chatbot, not traditional AR, not grindy, not a productivity tool.

3Research and planning

The core loop was defined up front: the alien appears, you have 5 seconds to say hi, you can talk for up to 90 seconds, and the encounter is saved to your collection. Timings were kept as named settings so they could be tuned without rework.

4Scope and feature decisions

  • Device strategy: Android phones that support WebXR get true floor placement. iPhone and desktop get a camera view with the alien on a fixed floor line. The 3D library only downloads on devices that can use it.
  • In scope: text or voice chat, a character that vaguely remembers past conversations, an encounter history.
  • Parked: multiple personalities, location-based spawns, achievements and community features.

5Integrations

  • Google Gemini drives the alien's cryptic, philosophical persona, with fallback lines if the AI is unavailable.
  • WebXR hit-testing finds a real floor point, and a DOM overlay keeps the chat interface usable inside AR.
  • Web Speech API for voice input and output; device camera; Web Audio for sound effects.

6Testing and iteration

An AR placement test and a calibration step were built into the app so floor detection could be checked on real devices. Early versions faked depth with CSS and device motion; later releases added real floor anchoring. A troubleshooting guide covers camera, microphone and browser-support issues.

7Deployment

Deployed on Vercel over HTTPS, which browsers require for camera and microphone access. The Gemini key is set as an environment variable.

8Lessons learned

Device fragmentation is a scope question as much as a technical one. Defining a good baseline for every phone, then enhancing where the device allows, meant the game shipped for everyone instead of waiting on one platform.

Case study 06 · Stakeholder pitch

60-Second Puzzle Fix: a concept pitch for Bauer Media

A timed puzzle widget produced as a concept pitch tailored to a specific media business: bite-sized daily brain teasers, one at a time, timed or relaxed.

My role
Concept owner: pitch framing, content formats, quality checks and release
Integrations
None external by design; runs entirely in the browser
Status
Live concept prototype
View live app (60-Second Puzzle Fix, opens in a new tab)

1The problem

Bauer's interactive puzzles page had been retired. Rather than describe what a replacement could be, the pitch shows one: a working sample the business can try in under a minute.

2The user

Two audiences. The stakeholder is the Bauer team reviewing the pitch, who need to grasp the idea instantly. The end user is a puzzle fan wanting a quick coffee-break challenge, in British English with familiar references.

3Research and planning

I planned five short formats (Wordsearch, Code Crack, Logic Snippet, Arithmetic and The Chain), three difficulty labels (Quick, Brisk, Sharp) and a 60-second timer. The visual style is an editorial navy-and-yellow look, closer to a magazine than a game.

4Scope and feature decisions

  • In scope: hints and explanations, score, streak and best time saved on the device, shuffle, and "Renew" to generate a brand-new puzzle of any type on demand.
  • Deliberately out: accounts, a backend and analytics. For a pitch, speed and zero setup mattered more.

5Integrations

None external. A built-in generator creates new puzzles, the browser stores progress, and Web Audio plays a short chime on success. With no keys, servers or databases, there's nothing to break during a demo.

6Testing and iteration

Content is checked against a seven-point editorial QA checklist shown in the app: clue accuracy, answer uniqueness, British English spelling and grammar, grid alignment, word and number counts, clear instructions for first-time solvers, and re-solving each puzzle from a blank state. A final pass removed leftover scaffolding before the pitch was shared.

7Deployment

A static build on Vercel: one link to send, loading instantly on any device.

8Lessons learned

Tailor the deliverable to the stakeholder. A working prototype in the client's own context makes the case faster than a slide deck, and showing the QA process builds trust in the content.
